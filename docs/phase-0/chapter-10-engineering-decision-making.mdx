# Chapter 10 — Engineering Decision Making

> **Good engineering decisions are not about proving that your option is perfect. They are about choosing a reasonable option for the current evidence, constraints, and trade-offs.**

---

## 1. The problem

Imagine your team has a large React application.

Build time has grown to 15 minutes.

Someone proposes:

> "Let's migrate from Webpack to Vite."

Engineer A says:

> Vite is faster. Let's migrate.

Engineer B says:

> Webpack already works. Don't touch it.

Engineer C says:

> Turbopack is the future.

Engineer D says:

> Let's migrate to Next.js while we're at it.

Who is correct?

Possibly none of them.

Because they started with **solutions**.

We need to go backward.

---

## 2. Start with the decision, not the technology

The actual problem might be:

> Developers are losing too much productive time waiting for builds.

Now we can investigate.

Maybe:

```
15-minute build
      ↓
Why?
      ↓
Webpack?
CI infrastructure?
Type checking?
Tests?
Dependency graph?
Poor caching?
```

What if Webpack itself only accounts for 90 seconds?

Migrating to Vite might not solve the real problem at all.

Chapter 7 returns:

> **Root Cause Analysis before solution selection.**

---

## 3. First principle of decision making

Why is engineering decision-making difficult?

Because we almost never have complete information.

Imagine you need to decide today whether to adopt Technology X.

You don't know:

- what requirements you'll have in two years
- whether the library will remain maintained
- whether your organization will restructure
- whether browsers will introduce something better
- whether the product will grow 10x
- whether the migration will uncover hidden problems

Yet you still need to make a decision.

This gives us a fundamental truth:

> **Engineering decisions are made under uncertainty.**

If we waited until we knew everything, we'd never build anything.

---

## 4. A decision is not a prediction

This distinction matters.

Suppose you choose Architecture A.

Two years later, requirements change and Architecture B becomes better.

Does that mean your original decision was wrong?

Not necessarily.

The correct question is:

> Given what we knew **at the time**, was A a reasonable choice?

Good engineering does not mean predicting the future perfectly.

It means making defensible decisions with the information currently available.

---

## 5. The decision framework

When facing an important technical decision, think:

```
Problem
   ↓
Goals
   ↓
Constraints
   ↓
Evidence
   ↓
Options
   ↓
Trade-offs
   ↓
Risks
   ↓
Reversibility
   ↓
Decision
   ↓
Measure
   ↓
Revisit if assumptions change
```

Let's understand each one.

---

## 6. Step 1 — Define the problem

Bad:

> Should we use Redux or Zustand?

Good:

> We have state shared across 30 components, and our current state architecture is becoming difficult to reason about.

See the difference?

The first question starts with technologies.

The second starts with the problem.

Another example:

Bad:

> Should we use microfrontends?

Good:

> Eight teams working in the same frontend cannot release independently without coordinating deployments.

Now we can reason.

---

## 7. Step 2 — Define the goal

A problem tells us what's wrong.

A goal tells us what success looks like.

Problem:

> CI takes 25 minutes.

Goal:

> Reduce median PR feedback time below 10 minutes without reducing test coverage.

Much better.

Now we can measure whether our solution worked.

Without a success criterion, teams often declare projects successful simply because the migration finished.

That's not enough.

---

## 8. Step 3 — Identify constraints

Suppose we're selecting a frontend framework.

Constraints might include:

```
Must support existing browsers
Must work with current authentication
Team knows React
Six-month deadline
Must support accessibility
Cannot rewrite backend
SEO required
```

Constraints eliminate options.

This connects directly to Chapter 9.

---

## 9. Step 4 — Gather evidence

Now investigate reality.

For our slow-build example:

```
Measure build stages
Measure dependency graph
Measure cache hit rate
Profile TypeScript
Profile tests
Profile bundling
Compare local vs CI
```

Don't make architecture decisions from feelings when relevant evidence is available.

Instead of:

> "Webpack feels slow."

We want:

> "Bundling accounts for 62% of our median CI build time."

Now we have evidence.

---

## 10. Step 5 — Generate multiple options

A common mistake is comparing:

```
My Solution
vs.
Do Nothing
```

That's weak decision-making.

Generate alternatives.

For example:

### Problem

Builds are too slow.

### Options

```
A — Improve Webpack configuration

B — Add better caching

C — Upgrade CI hardware

D — Migrate to Vite

E — Split the application

F — Change test strategy

G — Combination of several options
```

Now we're actually engineering.

---

## 11. Step 6 — Compare trade-offs

Suppose our simplified comparison is:

| Option               | Speed gain | Migration cost |   Risk | Long-term benefit |
| -------------------- | ---------: | -------------: | -----: | ----------------: |
| Improve caching      |     Medium |            Low |    Low |            Medium |
| Upgrade hardware     |     Medium |            Low |    Low |               Low |
| Vite migration       |       High |           High | Medium |              High |
| Rewrite architecture |  Very high |      Very high |   High |  Potentially high |

Notice:

There is no obvious "best."

The answer depends on constraints.

If you need improvement **this month**, caching might win.

If the platform will be maintained for another decade, migration may deserve more consideration.

Context determines the answer.

---

## 12. Step 7 — Evaluate risk

An option can look excellent on paper but contain substantial uncertainty.

Ask:

- What can fail?
- How likely is it?
- How bad would the impact be?
- Can we detect failure?
- Can we recover?

A useful mental model is:

```
Risk ≈ Probability × Impact
```

This isn't always something you need to calculate numerically. It's a way to reason.

For example:

### Risk A

Minor visual bug.

Probability: High
Impact: Low

### Risk B

Checkout stops processing payments.

Probability: Low
Impact: Catastrophic

Risk B may deserve much stronger safeguards even though it is less likely.

---

## 13. Reversible vs irreversible decisions

This is one of the most useful ideas in engineering leadership.

Not all decisions deserve the same amount of analysis.

### Reversible decision

Example:

> Change a linting rule.

If it doesn't work:

Undo it.

Don't spend three months debating.

### Expensive-to-reverse decision

Example:

> Move hundreds of applications onto a new platform architecture.

Changing your mind later could cost millions.

Spend much more time investigating.

Mental model:

```
Easy to reverse
     ↓
Decide quickly
Experiment
Learn


Hard to reverse
     ↓
Investigate deeply
Prototype
Gather evidence
Plan migration
```

The harder a decision is to reverse, the more evidence you generally want before committing.

---

## 14. One-way and two-way doors

A useful metaphor is doors.

### Two-way door

You walk through.

Don't like what's there?

Walk back.

Examples:

```
Component naming
Small library
Feature flag
Minor UI pattern
```

Make these decisions relatively quickly.

### One-way—or very expensive—door

You walk through and returning is difficult.

Examples:

```
Organization-wide platform
Database architecture
Public API contract
Major framework migration
Design-system API used by hundreds of apps
```

Slow down.

Gather evidence.

---

## 15. Prototypes reduce uncertainty

Suppose we're debating whether Vite can support our architecture.

We can argue for three weeks.

Or build:

```
Small Proof of Concept
       ↓
Test critical assumptions
       ↓
Gather evidence
       ↓
Make decision
```

A prototype is not necessarily intended to become production code.

Its purpose may simply be:

> **Reduce uncertainty cheaply.**

That's an important distinction.

---

## 16. Decision quality vs outcome quality

This is subtle.

Imagine:

You carefully evaluate an investment.

Everything suggests it is reasonable.

Then an unpredictable event causes it to fail.

Bad outcome.

Was the decision necessarily bad?

No.

Now reverse it.

Someone makes a reckless decision without evidence.

By luck, it succeeds.

Good outcome.

Was the decision good?

Still no.

So distinguish:

```
Decision Quality ≠ Outcome Quality
```

Evaluate decisions based on the reasoning and evidence available at the time, not only hindsight.

This prevents organizations from learning the wrong lessons.

---

## 17. Beware of sunk cost

Imagine your team has spent:

```
8 months
```

building Framework X.

Evidence now shows it isn't solving the problem.

Someone says:

> "We can't stop. We've already spent eight months."

That's dangerous reasoning.

Those eight months are gone regardless.

The better question is:

> Starting **today**, what choice gives us the best future outcome?

Past investment should inform what assets or migration costs remain, but shouldn't by itself justify continuing a bad direction.

---

## 18. Beware of technology enthusiasm

Engineers love technology.

That's useful—and dangerous.

Imagine:

> "Rust is amazing. Let's rewrite our frontend tooling in Rust."

Maybe that's a good idea.

But the decision should not be:

```
Technology is exciting
        ↓
Adopt technology
```

It should be:

```
Problem
↓
Constraints
↓
Requirements
↓
Options
↓
Evidence
↓
Trade-offs
↓
Decision
```

Tools serve problems.

Problems don't exist to justify tools.

---

## 19. Beware of resume-driven development

A dangerous pattern is choosing technologies because engineers want them on their résumé.

Example:

```
Simple internal dashboard

↓

Kubernetes

↓

Microservices

↓

Kafka

↓

GraphQL

↓

Microfrontends
```

Could these technologies work?

Sure.

Were they necessary?

Maybe not.

Remember Chapter 6:

> Every unnecessary technology adds relationships and therefore complexity.

Use the simplest architecture that satisfies the actual constraints while leaving appropriate room for likely evolution.

---

## 20. Decision matrices

For sufficiently important decisions, we can make reasoning explicit.

Suppose we're choosing a frontend build tool.

Criteria:

```
Development speed
Production build
Plugin ecosystem
Migration effort
Team familiarity
Long-term maintenance
```

We could compare options:

| Criterion              | Webpack | Vite | Alternative |
| ---------------------- | ------: | ---: | ----------: |
| Dev experience         |       2 |    5 |           4 |
| Existing compatibility |       5 |    3 |           2 |
| Migration effort       |       5 |    3 |           2 |
| Ecosystem fit          |       5 |    4 |           3 |
| Team knowledge         |       5 |    3 |           2 |

Don't blindly add numbers and declare a winner.

The matrix exists to **make assumptions visible**.

Someone can challenge:

> Why is compatibility more important than developer speed?

Excellent.

Now we're discussing priorities instead of opinions.

---

## 21. Separate facts, assumptions, and preferences

This is a powerful habit.

During an architecture discussion, write:

### Facts

```
Current CI median = 22 minutes
120 applications use current tooling
20 custom plugins exist
```

### Assumptions

```
Application count will increase
Vite migration will improve local startup
Teams can migrate incrementally
```

### Preferences

```
Team prefers Vite
Developers dislike Webpack configuration
```

These are not equally strong evidence.

A common engineering failure is presenting an assumption as though it were a fact.

---

## 22. Make assumptions explicit

Suppose we decide:

> We'll use client-side rendering because SEO isn't required.

Write that assumption down.

Six months later:

Marketing says:

> We need Google indexing.

Now we know exactly which assumption changed.

Architecture decisions become much easier to revisit when their assumptions are documented.

---

## 23. Decisions have a time horizon

Architecture doesn't need to solve every hypothetical future problem.

Imagine your startup has:

```
100 users
3 engineers
```

Designing immediately for:

```
1 billion users
10,000 engineers
```

may create enormous accidental complexity.

But ignoring obvious growth entirely is also dangerous.

The question is:

> **What future should we reasonably design for?**

This is judgment.

There is no formula that removes it.

---

## 24. Staff engineers optimize for optionality

Here's a more advanced concept.

Suppose you're uncertain about the future.

Instead of predicting perfectly, sometimes you design the system so multiple futures remain possible.

For example:

```
Application
     ↓
Stable interface
     ↓
Implementation
```

If the implementation can later be replaced without changing every consumer, you've preserved **optionality**.

Abstraction returns.

Good abstractions can reduce the cost of future change.

But remember Chapter 6:

Too much abstraction "just in case" creates complexity.

Again:

trade-off.

---

## 25. When should we delay a decision?

Sometimes the best decision is:

> **Not yet.**

If waiting gives you valuable information and doesn't create major cost, delay can be rational.

Example:

You don't yet know whether your application needs global state management.

Don't install Redux merely because it might.

Start simpler.

When the requirement becomes clear, decide with better evidence.

This is sometimes called delaying commitment until the **last responsible moment**—not the last possible moment.

The difference matters.

---

## 26. When should we decide quickly?

When:

- The decision is reversible.
- Cost of delay is high.
- Additional research won't materially change the answer.
- The stakes are low.

Staff engineers aren't slow decision-makers.

They know **which decisions deserve slowness**.

## 27. Architecture Decision Records

Now we're finally ready for something we'll use throughout this course.

An **ADR** records an important architecture decision.

Basic structure:

```
ADR-001

Title:
Use Vite as Application Build Tool

Status:
Accepted

Context:
What problem exists?

Constraints:
What limitations matter?

Options:
What alternatives did we consider?

Decision:
What did we choose?

Rationale:
Why?

Consequences:
What do we gain?
What do we sacrifice?

Assumptions:
What must remain true?

Revisit when:
What would cause us to reconsider?
```

Notice how almost all of Phase 0 appears inside an ADR.

That's not an accident.

---

## 28. Example ADR

### Context

Local development startup takes 90 seconds and significantly slows feedback.

### Goal

Reduce startup below 10 seconds.

### Options

```
Optimize Webpack
Vite
Alternative bundler
Do nothing
```

### Decision

Suppose we choose Vite.

### Why

```
Measured startup improvement
Acceptable compatibility
Incremental migration possible
Strong ecosystem fit
```

### Consequences

Benefits:

```
Faster feedback
Simpler dev configuration
```

Costs:

```
Migration effort
Plugin rewrites
Training
New operational knowledge
```

### Revisit when

```
Critical compatibility requirement appears
Maintenance becomes uncertain
Platform constraints change
```

That's engineering documentation.

Not:

> "We chose Vite because it's fast."

## Mental Model

Imagine you're crossing a river.

There are several paths:

```
Bridge

Boat

Swim

Go around
```

There is no universally correct option.

You need to know:

```
How deep is the river?
How much time do you have?
Can you swim?
How much money do you have?
Are you carrying equipment?
Is there a bridge nearby?
```

The correct decision emerges from **context**.

Software architecture works the same way.

---

## Common Mistakes

- Starting with a preferred technology.
- Considering only one option.
- Treating assumptions as facts.
- Ignoring migration cost.
- Ignoring reversibility.
- Overanalyzing trivial decisions.
- Underanalyzing expensive decisions.
- Letting sunk cost control future choices.
- Measuring decision quality only by eventual outcome.
- Designing for imaginary scale without evidence.

---

## Chapter Summary

Engineering decision-making is the process of selecting a reasonable course of action despite incomplete information.

Strong engineers:

- define the problem first
- establish measurable goals
- identify constraints
- gather evidence
- generate alternatives
- compare trade-offs
- evaluate risk
- consider reversibility
- document assumptions
- measure outcomes
- revisit decisions when circumstances change

The goal isn't certainty.

> **The goal is a defensible decision.**

```

```
Easy to reverse

## Interview Perspective

### Junior

**How would you choose between two libraries?**

Don't answer only with popularity.

Discuss requirements, maintenance, ecosystem, complexity, and fit.

### Mid-level

**Redux or Context—which should we use?**

Correct mindset:

> What state problem are we trying to solve?

Then reason from requirements.

### Senior

**Should we migrate from Webpack to Vite?**

Discuss:

- measured problem
- migration cost
- compatibility
- developer productivity
- alternatives
- incremental migration
- success metrics

### Staff

**Twenty teams disagree on the frontend platform direction. How do you make the decision?**

A strong answer includes:

```
Shared problem definition
Explicit constraints
Data
Stakeholder requirements
Alternatives
Prototype
Decision criteria
Risks
Migration strategy
Documentation
Success metrics
Revisit conditions
```

The staff engineer's job isn't simply to win the argument.

It's to create a process through which the organization can reach and execute a good decision.
